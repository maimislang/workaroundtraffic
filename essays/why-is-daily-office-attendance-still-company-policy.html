Why is daily office attendance still company policy in the Philippines?

By Mai G. Mislang · October 4, 2026

Ask most Philippine organizations why employees must be at their desks five days a week, and the answer is rarely a reason. It is a reflex. Daily attendance is treated as a law of nature — the way work simply is. It is not. It is a policy, and policies can be changed.

The default nobody actually chose

Fixed, full-week attendance is an inheritance from the factory floor, where output really did depend on bodies standing in one place at one time. Most knowledge work does not function that way. Yet the habit survived the shift from assembly line to spreadsheet, and presence quietly became a stand-in for productivity. We started measuring who is seen rather than what is done.

No one sat down and decided this was the best design for a city like Metro Manila. It was never designed at all. It was simply never questioned.

The bill lands on your people

The cost of that unexamined default does not show up on a balance sheet. It shows up in your team. In Metro Manila, the commute alone can take two to four hours a day — time subtracted from rest, family, and the focus that good work requires. The book Unstuck calls this the bandwidth tax: the cognitive fuel the commute burns before the workday even begins.

Over a year, those hours add up to weeks of a person's life spent in transit. The people who pay it most are often the ones you can least afford to lose. Attrition, burnout, and quiet disengagement are not separate problems. They are the invoice for a policy that was never chosen on purpose.

The law is already on your side

Here is what many leaders do not realize: the barrier is not the law. The Telecommuting Act — Republic Act No. 11165, signed in 2018 — already recognizes telecommuting as a legitimate alternative work arrangement in the private sector. It is voluntary, agreed on between employer and employee, and it protects flexible workers with treatment comparable to their onsite colleagues.

In other words, the permission slip has existed for years. What is missing is not legality. It is the policy work — deciding which roles can flex, proving the business case, and running a disciplined pilot instead of making a leap. That work is doable, and it does not require waiting for the government to fix the roads first.
